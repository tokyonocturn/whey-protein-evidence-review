# How much protein, and how big should a scoop be?

## The reference points

| Body | Recommendation | Source |
|---|---|---|
| US/Canadian RDA | 0.8 g/kg/day | Nitrogen balance; minimum to prevent deficiency, not optimum |
| Acceptable range for adults | 10-35% of energy | Dietary Guidelines |
| Athletes (ACSM/RD) | 1.2-2.0 g/kg/day | Position stand |
| Resistance training, hypertrophy | 1.6 g/kg/day, up to 2.2 g/kg/day | Morton 2018 [S01]; Schoenfeld & Aragon [S04] |
| Per meal for MPS | 0.25-0.4 g/kg, or 20-40 g | [S04, S09] |
| Older adults, healthy | 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day | ESPEN/PROT-AGE [S16] |
| Older adults, illness | 1.2-1.5 g/kg/day | ESPEN [S16] |
| Chronic kidney disease G3-G5 | 0.8 g/kg/day; avoid >1.3 | KDIGO 2024 [S13] |
| Cirrhosis | 1.2-1.5 g/kg ideal body weight/day | EASL/AASLD [S14, S15] |

The RDA is a floor, not a target. It is the intake below which deficiency develops in most people.
It is not the intake that maximises muscle, preserves muscle in ageing, or supports athletic
performance.

## The 1.6 g/kg finding, correctly understood

Morton's breakpoint of **1.62 g/kg/day** is the most quoted number in sports nutrition, and it is
routinely misquoted as "the maximum the body can use". What the analysis actually shows is that
**additional protein beyond ~1.6 g/kg/day did not produce further resistance-training-induced gains
in fat-free mass on average**. A 95% confidence interval of 1.03-2.20 means the true breakpoint for
any individual could be higher. Some people, especially lean, trained, in a calorie deficit, or
older, benefit from 2.0-2.2 g/kg/day.

The practical range for a strength athlete is therefore **1.6-2.2 g/kg/day**, with 1.6 as the
efficient target and 2.2 as the upper end used in the literature. Higher intakes are not harmful in
healthy people (doc 11); they are simply not more anabolic.

## Per-meal dosing

Muscle protein synthesis responds to dose, then saturates. The best-supported approach is
**0.4 g/kg per meal across at least four meals**, which reaches about 1.6 g/kg/day; at 2.2 g/kg/day,
0.55 g/kg per meal [S04]. In absolute terms, 20-40 g of high-quality protein per meal, with 25-30 g
being a sensible default for most adults.

For a 70 kg person: 28 g per meal; for a 90 kg person: 36 g; for a 110 kg person: 44 g. Older adults
should aim at the upper end (25-30 g) because of anabolic resistance and the leucine threshold.

## What that means in scoops

Our dataset's products deliver 20-30 g of protein per serving, with most at 22-25 g. One scoop is
therefore a well-sized dose for a 60-90 kg adult. Larger people, or people using a 20 g isolate, may
need 1.5 scoops to hit 0.4 g/kg.

Daily protein targets and scoop equivalents (at 24 g per scoop):

| Body weight | 1.2 g/kg | 1.6 g/kg | 2.0 g/kg | Scoops at 1.6 g/kg |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 60 kg | 72 g | 96 g | 120 g | 4.0 |
| 75 kg | 90 g | 120 g | 150 g | 5.0 |
| 90 kg | 108 g | 144 g | 180 g | 6.0 |
| 110 kg | 132 g | 176 g | 220 g | 7.3 |

Those scoop counts are **not** a recommendation to drink seven shakes. They show how much of the
target a supplement can plausibly cover. Food should supply most of it; one or two scoops is the
realistic supplement pattern.

## Should you use body weight or lean mass?

For people with obesity, dosing on total body weight can produce unnecessarily high targets. Using
**target body weight** or **lean body mass plus an allowance** is common clinical practice. A
practical compromise: use current weight up to a BMI of about 30, then use weight at BMI 30 or
adjusted body weight. There is no single validated equation; the direction of the adjustment is what
matters.

## Timing

- **Total daily intake is the main lever** [S01].
- The post-exercise "window" is hours wide, not 30 minutes. Protein within a few hours of training
  is fine.
- Spacing protein across 3-5 meals is more useful than concentrating it in one.
- **Pre-sleep protein** (about 40 g casein or a slow blend) increases overnight MPS in some trials;
  the practical benefit for hypertrophy is small and not firmly established.
- **Pre-meal whey** (10-40 g before a carbohydrate meal) lowers postprandial glucose, with the
  largest effect in type 2 diabetes [S41]. This is a clinical use, not a bodybuilding one.

## Protein in a calorie deficit

When cutting, higher protein (2.0-2.4 g/kg/day) helps preserve lean mass and increases satiety. This
is one of the few situations where intakes above 1.6 g/kg are clearly justified. The same applies
during aggressive weight loss in older adults, where 1.2-1.5 g/kg is recommended to protect muscle.

## Protein for older adults

Aim for **1.0-1.2 g/kg/day** when healthy and **1.2-1.5 g/kg/day** during illness, with 25-30 g per
meal and at least one meal containing a rapidly digested, leucine-rich protein [S04, S16]. A whey shake is
a practical way to add 25 g to a breakfast that would otherwise be carbohydrate-only.

## What "excessive" means

There is no defined upper limit for protein in healthy adults. Intakes of 2.5-3.3 g/kg/day have been
studied for a year without harmful changes in kidney or liver markers [S08, S09]. "Excessive" is
better defined by context than by a number:

- **No concern:** 1.2-2.2 g/kg/day in healthy, active people.
- **Unnecessary but not harmful:** 2.2-3.3 g/kg/day in healthy people; the extra is oxidised or
  excreted, and it displaces other foods.
- **Requires medical supervision:** any high-protein intake in CKD, liver failure, disorders of
  protein metabolism, or before bariatric surgery.
- **Actually excessive:** intakes that crowd out fibre, vegetables, unsaturated fats and total
  energy balance, or that rely on supplements instead of food.

## A note on "30 g per meal max"

The claim that the body can only use 20-30 g of protein per meal is wrong. Higher doses do produce
more amino acid oxidation, but they also continue to support tissue building; the 0.4 g/kg per meal
recommendation is about efficiency, not a hard ceiling [S04]. A 60 g protein meal is not wasted, it
is just less efficient per gram.

## Bottom line

Target **1.6 g/kg/day** (range 1.2-2.2 depending on goal and population), split over **3-5 meals**,
with **25-30 g of high-quality protein per meal**. One scoop of most whey products is 22-25 g, which
makes it a convenient way to fix one inadequate meal, not a reason to restructure your diet around
powder.
