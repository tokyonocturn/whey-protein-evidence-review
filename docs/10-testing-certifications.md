# Independent testing and third-party certification

Certification is the only consumer-facing signal that someone outside the manufacturer has looked at
the product. It is also widely misunderstood: different programmes test different things, against
different limits, at different frequencies. "Third-party tested" on a tub can mean anything from
every batch screened for 290 banned substances to a certificate of analysis the brand wrote itself.

## The programmes that matter

| Programme | Operator | What is tested | Frequency | Banned substances | Public lookup |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **NSF Certified for Sport** | NSF International | Label vs product, heavy metals, microbes, mycotoxins, pesticides, 290+ banned substances, facility audit | Product and production lots, ongoing | Yes (WADA, NFL, MLB) | Product and lot |
| **NSF/ANSI 173 (Contents Certified)** | NSF International | Content vs label, contaminants | Product | No | Product |
| **Informed Sport** | LGC Group | 285+ prohibited substances in ISO 17025 labs, plus manufacturing review | **Every batch** | Yes | Batch |
| **Informed Choice** | LGC Group | Random-lot screening for prohibited substances | At least 12 lots/year | Yes | Tested lots |
| **USP Verified** | USP | Identity, potency, contaminants, dissolution, facility audit, off-the-shelf retest | Product | No | Product |
| **BSCG Certified Drug Free** | BSCG | Banned substances plus contaminants | Batch | Yes | Certificate |
| **Clean Label Project Certified** | Clean Label Project | Heavy metals, bisphenols, pesticides against Prop 65-linked benchmarks | Product/batch | No | Mark |
| **Labdoor** | Labdoor (private) | Label accuracy and heavy metals, graded A-F | Product sample | No | Grades |
| **Brand COA** | Manufacturer | Whatever the brand chooses | Voluntary | No | Maybe |

## What each programme can and cannot tell you

### NSF Certified for Sport

The strictest widely available programme. It combines a facility audit, label verification,
contaminant testing and a 290-substance banned-substance screen, and NSF lists the specific products
and lots it certified. For a drug-tested athlete, this is the gold standard.

It cannot tell you that a product is low in lead against *Consumer Reports'* 0.5 µg/day benchmark,
because the programme uses its own limits. Certification is not a guarantee of passing a stricter
external benchmark.

### Informed Sport

Batch-level testing is its distinguishing feature: every production batch is screened before release,
which is more protective than testing a product once a year. LGC's programme is recognised by UK
Anti-Doping and many professional teams. If a product carries Informed Sport and you can check the
batch code, you have the strongest assurance available that the tub in your hand was screened.

### Informed Choice

Same laboratory and methods, different frequency: random lots rather than every batch, plus retail
monitoring. Weaker than Informed Sport, still meaningful.

### USP Verified

Strong on identity, potency and contaminants, with an off-the-shelf retest. Very few protein powders
participate, which limits its usefulness in this category.

### Labdoor

Useful secondary signal, but it grades a sample, not a production run, and its methodology and sample
dates are not always fully disclosed. Treat an A grade as weak positive evidence, not a certification.

### Brand COAs

A certificate of analysis is only as good as the laboratory that produced it and the lot it refers
to. A COA without an accredited lab name, a lot number and a date is marketing.

## The gap certification does not close

Three problems remain even with certification:

1. **Different limits.** A product can pass a certification's contaminant screen and still exceed
   Prop 65's lead MADL, because the benchmarks differ.
2. **Product vs batch.** A certified product tested last year may be made in a different facility
   this year. Informed Sport's batch model is the best answer to this; product-level certification is
   not.
3. **Protein quantity vs protein quality.** Certification verifies that the label's protein number is
   accurate by nitrogen analysis. It does not verify that the nitrogen came from intact whey rather
   than added amino acids. Amino-acid spiking can coexist with a passing label test.

## Independent surveys of label accuracy

Certification is not the only evidence. Retail surveys consistently find a minority of products
off-label:

- **LGC's May 2025 UK survey** of 41 protein products found **11 below their label claim**, most
  within 5%, one at about half the stated amount.
- **The Citizens Protein Project** (India, 2024) found **69.4% of 36 products** below label, with
  deficits from under 10% to over 50%. Small, non-random sample, but a warning about market
  variation.
- **Consumer Reports 2025** found all 23 tested products met or exceeded their protein claim. That is
  reassuring for the US mass market, and consistent with better enforcement and larger brands.

The pattern: label accuracy is generally good in regulated Western markets and much worse in
poorly enforced ones. Buying a brand with a certification register entry is the simplest protection.

## How to verify a product yourself

1. Search the certifier's public register for the exact product name (NSF: product and lot; Informed
   Sport: batch).
2. Check the lot code on the tub against the register, not just the product name.
3. Read the ingredient list for free amino acids (spiking signal) and for the protein source order.
4. Divide protein grams by scoop grams: below about 65% suggests a diluted or blended product.
5. Look for a published heavy-metal result. "Non-detect" from an independent lab is stronger than
   "tested for purity".

## Bottom line

For drug-tested athletes, **Informed Sport (batch) or NSF Certified for Sport** are the only
certifications that provide meaningful assurance, and they should be verified by lot. For everyone
else, certification is a useful quality signal but not a guarantee of low heavy metals; product-level
test data [S30, S31] is more informative about contamination. A brand COA is not certification.
