# Manufacturing quality, recalls and regulation

Protein powder sits in a regulatory gap: it is a food or a dietary supplement, not a drug, so it is
marketed without pre-market approval and tested only sporadically. That does not make it unsafe, but
it explains why label accuracy and contamination vary so much between products.

## How whey powder is made

1. **Cheese or casein manufacture** produces sweet whey (or acid whey).
2. **Clarification and pasteurisation** remove fat and curd fines and reduce microbial load.
3. **Ultrafiltration / diafiltration** concentrates protein and removes lactose, ash and water.
4. **Ion exchange or microfiltration** (for isolate) raises protein above 90%.
5. **Spray drying** turns the concentrate into powder; inlet/outlet temperatures affect protein
   denaturation and lysine availability.
6. **Agglomeration and blending** add flavour, sweetener, lecithin, gums and sometimes enzymes.
7. **Filling and packaging** into tubs or bags, with lot coding.

Critical control points are heat damage (which reduces available lysine), environmental pathogens
(*Salmonella*, *Listeria*) in the drying and filling areas, allergen cross-contact, and the temptation
to add cheap nitrogen.

## The regulatory framework

| Issue | Rule | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Classification | Dietary supplement (DSHEA) or conventional food | No pre-market approval; manufacturer responsible for safety |
| Manufacturing | 21 CFR 111 (supplements) or 21 CFR 117 (food cGMP) | Facility and process controls, hazard analysis |
| Labels | Supplement Facts or Nutrition Facts; all ingredients declared | Protein must be declared where present |
| Protein Daily Value | 50 g | A 25 g serving is 50% DV |
| Protein quality | 21 CFR 101.9(c)(7): corrected for amino acid score and digestibility when a protein claim is made | Rarely enforced for powders |
| Amino-acid-only products | 21 CFR 101.36: protein **shall not** be declared | A hook against spiking |
| Structure/function claims | Permitted with substantiation and a disclaimer; not pre-approved | "Supports muscle recovery" is allowed; "treats sarcopenia" is not |
| Heavy metals | No enforceable lead limit specific to protein powders | CR petitioned FDA in October 2025 |
| Banned substances | No mandatory testing | Voluntary certification only |

The practical result: a product can be legal, meet its label, and still contain more lead than a
consumer group considers safe, or declare protein that comes partly from glycine.

## Recalls and enforcement actions

| Date | Company | Product | Issue | Action |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2011 | Vitalabs | Whey protein isolates | Possible *Salmonella* | Class 2 recall (CFIA/FDA) |
| 2018 | AMPI | Dry whey powder | Potential *Salmonella* | Precautionary recall; shipped product tested negative |
| 2021 | OFP Ingredients | Dry milk powder | *Salmonella* Cubana in facility environment; inadequate sanitation controls | FDA warning letter |
| 2024 | Orgain | Plant protein, chocolate | Undeclared peanut residue in one batch | Voluntary allergy alert and recall |
| 2026 | Hearthy Foods | Ready-to-eat whey protein isolate | Adulterated: insanitary conditions; no hazard analysis for *Salmonella*/*Listeria* recontamination | FDA warning letter |

The Hearthy Foods warning letter is the most instructive recent document. FDA found that the
facility processed ready-to-eat whey isolate exposed to the environment and handled by employees,
had not identified environmental pathogens as a hazard requiring a preventive control, and showed
the investigator an example of reconditioned packaged product. That is a failure of process control,
not a one-off contamination event.

## The amino-acid spiking litigation

Two cases define the public record:

- **Gubala v. CVS Pharmacy (N.D. Ill. 2014-16).** Plaintiff alleged a "26 grams of high-quality
  protein" claim contained about **21.8 g of whey protein**, with the remainder from free amino acids.
  The court denied CVS's motion to dismiss, allowing the misleading-labelling claim to proceed.
- **Porter v. NBTY (N.D. Ill. 2015-19).** Plaintiffs alleged roughly **28%** of Body Fortress's
  labelled protein came from glycine, threonine, glutamine, leucine, valine, isoleucine, taurine and
  creatine. The court allowed the core false-advertising claims to proceed and dismissed others; the
  case was terminated in December 2019 without a public judgment or disclosed settlement.

Neither case produced a judicial finding that the products were adulterated. But the legal theory is
sound and the regulatory hook exists: 21 CFR 101.36 prohibits protein declarations on products that
contain only individual amino acids, and 21 CFR 101.9 requires corrected protein content when a
protein claim is made.

## Label accuracy in the wider market

- **US (Consumer Reports 2025):** all 23 products met or exceeded their protein claim.
- **UK (LGC 2025):** 11 of 41 products below claim; most within 5%, one at about half.
- **India (Citizens Protein Project 2024):** 69.4% of 36 products below claim, deficits up to 50%+.

The pattern is enforcement-dependent. Large Western brands are usually accurate; the long tail is
not.

## What manufacturing quality looks like from the outside

You cannot audit a factory from a tub, but you can read signals:

1. **Certification** (NSF Certified for Sport, Informed Sport) implies a facility audit.
2. **A published lot-specific COA** from an accredited lab is better than a generic "tested" claim.
3. **Ingredient list order** should put whey protein first in a whey product.
4. **Protein density** (protein grams / scoop grams) should be 70-85% for isolate and blend products;
   below 65% suggests dilution.
5. **No individual amino acids** in a whey product unless the product is explicitly an amino acid
   blend.
6. **Allergen panel** should match the flavour (wheat in cookies, peanut in chocolate peanut butter).
7. **Recall history** is public; a brand with repeated pathogen recalls deserves caution.

## Supply chain risk

Whey ingredients are traded globally as WPC 34/80 and WPI 90. A brand may buy from different dryers
in different countries across lots. This is why:

- Batch-to-batch consistency varies more than brand marketing implies.
- Informed Sport's every-batch model is more protective than product certification.
- A product that tested clean last year may not be the same ingredient source this year.

## Bottom line

The category is legal, mostly well-manufactured, and unevenly tested. Pathogen recalls in whey are
rare but real, and the 2026 FDA warning letter shows that environmental pathogen controls can fail.
Amino-acid spiking is a documented commercial practice with active litigation. The consumer's best
protection is a certified product with published contaminant testing, a sensible protein density, and
an ingredient list short enough to read.
