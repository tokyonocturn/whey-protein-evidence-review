# Grass-fed, organic, conventional and other marketed varieties

Whey is a by-product. Its quality depends on the milk, the filtration, and the drying, and the
labels that sell it are mostly about the cow, not the protein. This document separates the claims
that change the product from the claims that change the price.

## What the labels mean

### Organic

USDA Organic (National Organic Program) requires:

- No synthetic pesticides, synthetic fertilisers, sewage sludge or irradiation on the farm.
- No antibiotics or added growth hormones.
- Feed grown organically.
- Outdoor access and pasture for ruminants (with detailed rules).
- Annual inspection and a certification fee.

Organic is a **farming-process standard**. It does not test the finished powder for heavy metals, and
it does not change the amino acid profile of whey.

### Grass-fed

There is **no single legal definition or mandatory US standard for "grass-fed" dairy**. Several
third-party programmes exist (American Grassfed Association, Pennsylvania Certified Organic, Land to
Market) with different rules on pasture percentage, grain supplementation and confinement. A
"grass-fed" label without a certifier's mark is a marketing statement, not a verified standard.

Grass-fed whey is usually also higher in price because pasture-based milk costs more to produce, not
because the protein is superior.

### rBGH / rBST-free

Recombinant bovine somatotropin is a production hormone approved by the FDA in 1993. The FDA's
position is that there is **no significant difference** between milk from treated and untreated cows,
and labels claiming "from cows not treated with rBST" must carry that disclaimer (Optimum Nutrition
prints exactly this on its product page). The claim is about production practice, not nutrition or
safety.

### Native whey

Native whey is filtered from skim milk rather than recovered from cheese production, so it avoids the
cheese vat's heat and acid. It retains more intact beta-lactoglobulin, alpha-lactalbumin, lactoferrin
and immunoglobulins, and in some comparisons a higher leucine content. Trials show a faster and
higher leucinaemia than WPC 80, but **no difference in muscle protein synthesis** in the head-to-head
trial [S45]. It is a legitimate premium product with a weak outcome advantage.

### Microfiltered vs ion exchange

Both are valid ways to make isolate. Ion exchange uses charged resins and can denature more protein
and reduce some bioactive fractions; microfiltration is gentler and retains more lactoferrin. For
muscle protein synthesis and amino acid content, the difference is negligible. "Undenatured" is a
marketing term: some denaturation is unavoidable in any spray-drying process.

## What actually differs nutritionally

### Fatty acids

Organic and grass-fed milk have a better fatty acid profile:

- Organic milk: **25% less omega-6, 62% more omega-3**, 2.5-fold lower omega-6:omega-3 ratio, 18%
  more CLA in a US nationwide 18-month study [S37].
- A meta-analysis of 170 studies: organic milk had **56% more n-3 PUFA and 41% more CLA**, plus more
  alpha-tocopherol and iron, but **lower iodine and selenium** [S38].
- Grass-fed ("grassmilk") had about **0.05 g omega-3 per 100 g** versus 0.02 g in conventional milk, a
  147% relative increase [UMN Extension].

Those numbers sound impressive until you convert them to a scoop. **Whey isolate is 0.5-1% fat.** A
30 g scoop contains at most 0.3 g of fat, of which omega-3 is a fraction of a milligram. The fatty
acid advantage of grass-fed or organic milk is essentially eliminated by making isolate. Even a
concentrate at 4-8% fat delivers only 1-2 g of fat per scoop.

If you want the fatty acid benefit, drink milk or eat dairy fat. Do not buy grass-fed whey for it.

### Protein and amino acids

No meaningful difference. The amino acid profile of whey is determined by bovine genetics and the
casein:whey ratio, not by what the cow ate. Grass-fed and organic whey have the same DIAAS, the same
leucine content within analytical variation, and the same effect on MPS.

### Contaminants

Organic does **not** mean cleaner. The Clean Label Project found organic protein powders had **three
times the lead** of non-organic products, driven by plant ingredients, and 79% of organic products
exceeded the Prop 65 lead benchmark [S30]. For whey specifically, organic certification says nothing
about heavy metals.

### Hormones and antibiotics

Organic milk has no added hormones and no antibiotic residues by rule. Conventional US milk is
already tested for antibiotic residues and must be below tolerance; rBGH is not used in most US milk
and is not permitted in the EU, Canada, Japan, Australia and New Zealand. The practical difference is
small.

## The price premium

Comparing like-for-like cost per 25 g of protein from `data/derived_costs.csv`:

| Product | Claim | Cost per 25 g | Premium vs ON Gold Standard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Optimum Nutrition Gold Standard | conventional blend | $1.51 | - |
| Naked Whey | grass-fed concentrate | $1.84 | +22% |
| Transparent Labs | grass-fed isolate, NSF Sport | $1.93 | +28% |
| Garden of Life Sport | organic, grass-fed, NSF Sport | $2.19 | +45% |
| Legion Whey+ | grass-fed isolate, stevia | $2.27 | +50% |

You are paying 22-50% more for a farming claim, a certification, a sweetener preference, or all
three. Sometimes that is worth it. It is not worth it for protein quality.

## Other claims you will see

| Claim | What it means | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "Cold-processed" | Lower heat during processing | Plausible for bioactive fractions, no outcome evidence |
| "Undenatured" | Retains protein structure | Marketing; some denaturation is inevitable |
| "Predigested" | Hydrolysed | Faster absorption, no proven hypertrophy advantage |
| "Low lactose" | Usually isolate or lactase-treated | Meaningful for lactose sensitivity |
| "Keto-friendly" | Low carb per serving | Check total carbs, not the label claim |
| "No artificial sweeteners" | Stevia/monk fruit | Preference; not a safety difference |
| "Banned substance tested" | Could mean a full certification or an in-house screen | Verify which |
| "Informed Sport" | Every batch tested | Meaningful |
| "Grass-fed" with no certifier | Unverified | Treat as marketing |
| "Made in a GMP facility" | Facility standard, not product testing | Weak signal |

## Who should pay for the premium

**Worth it if:**

- You want to avoid synthetic pesticides and antibiotics as a values choice (organic).
- You want stevia or monk fruit instead of sucralose (Transparent Labs, Legion, Ascent, Thorne).
- You want a sport certification on a grass-fed isolate (Transparent Labs, Momentous).
- You care about pasture-based animal welfare.
- You want a single-ingredient product (Naked Whey).

**Not worth it if:**

- You are buying it for extra omega-3 or CLA. The fat is removed.
- You are buying it for "better protein". The amino acids are the same.
- You are buying it for lower heavy metals. Organic is not protective.
- You are on a budget. A conventional WPC or blend delivers the same protein at 20-40% less.

## Bottom line

Grass-fed and organic whey are real products with real farming differences, modest fatty acid
advantages in the milk, and no meaningful protein advantage in the powder. They cost 20-50% more.
Buy them for values, certification, or sweetener preference, not for muscle, and not for purity.
