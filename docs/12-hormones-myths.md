# Hormonal effects and other commonly circulated claims

Whey attracts more mythology than almost any supplement. This document separates what has been
measured from what has been inferred, repeated, or invented.

## Testosterone and estrogen

**Claim: whey raises estrogen / causes gynecomastia.**
There is no credible evidence for this. In a 12-week randomised trial in previously untrained men,
whey protein concentrate increased total testosterone pre- to post-training more than soy or placebo,
while **17-beta-estradiol was unchanged** in all groups [S24]. Estradiol does not rise with whey
supplementation in controlled studies. Gynecomastia cases attributed to supplements usually involve
anabolic steroids, prohormones, or contaminated products, not whey.

**Claim: whey boosts testosterone.**
Not meaningfully. Resistance training and adequate energy intake affect testosterone more than
protein source. The small training-related rise in the trial above was not a pharmacological effect.

## IGF-1

Dairy intake raises circulating IGF-1 modestly, within the normal range. This is a nutritional effect
of protein and calcium intake, not a doping effect, and it is seen with all animal proteins. In the
PROVIDE trial, a vitamin D, calcium and leucine-enriched whey supplement increased IGF-1 in
sarcopenic older adults alongside improvements in bone markers. There is no evidence that this
translates into increased cancer risk at normal dietary intakes; the epidemiological literature on
dairy and IGF-1 is inconsistent and confounded.

**Claim: whey is "growth hormone" or "steroid-like".** False. Whey contains no hormones at
pharmacological doses. Bovine somatotropin (rBGH) is a production hormone given to cows, not a
component of milk at biologically active levels in humans, and its use is addressed in doc 13.

## Insulin

**Claim: whey spikes insulin and makes you fat.**
Whey is insulinotropic: it raises insulin more than other proteins, because its amino acids
(especially leucine and the branched-chain amino acids) stimulate beta cells. That is a normal
physiological response and is the mechanism by which a whey premeal lowers postprandial glucose by
about **1.4 mmol/L** [S41]. In a calorie-controlled diet, whey does not cause fat gain; in fact,
higher-protein diets tend to improve body composition. Postprandial insulin spikes are not inherently
harmful in healthy people.

## Acne

This one is genuinely contested and worth taking seriously.

- A case-control study in Jordan found **47% of acne cases** used whey supplements versus **27.7% of
  controls** (p = 0.0047), and the association survived multivariable adjustment [S22].
- A 6-month double-blind randomised trial in men with acne found whey supplementation was
  **noninferior** to no whey for changes in lesion counts and severity [S21]. That is, it did not
  make acne meaningfully worse in a controlled setting.
- A meta-analysis of dairy and acne (14 studies, 78,529 participants) found any dairy associated with
  an odds ratio of **1.25** for acne, with skim milk at 1.32 [S23]. Observational only.

The mechanism proposed is insulin/IGF-1-mediated sebum production. Practical reading: whey may worsen
acne in some susceptible young men, the controlled evidence is weaker than the observational
association, and a trial of eliminating whey is reasonable for someone with stubborn acne. Do not
expect a dramatic effect.

## "You can only absorb 30 g of protein per meal"

Wrong. Higher doses increase amino acid oxidation, but they continue to support protein synthesis.
The 0.4 g/kg per meal recommendation is about efficiency, not a hard ceiling [S04].

## "Whey is wasted without a workout within 30 minutes"

Wrong. The anabolic window is hours wide, and total daily protein intake is a far stronger predictor
of hypertrophy than timing [S01].

## "Hydrolysed whey is twice as anabolic"

Wrong. Hydrolysate raises blood amino acids faster and produced similar MPS to intact whey in a
direct randomised comparison (+67% vs +57%, not statistically different) [S06]. It may help recovery
in some protocols, but it is not twice as anabolic.

## "Whey causes kidney damage"

Only in people who already have kidney disease. See doc 11 [S10, S11, S13].

## "Whey leaches calcium from bones"

Not supported. Higher protein intake is associated with higher bone mineral density and lower hip
fracture risk in cohorts, and supplementation trials show no harm [S42, S43].

## "Whey damages the liver"

No evidence in healthy people at studied intakes, including 3.3 g/kg/day for a year [S08].

## "BCAAs are the active ingredient, so BCAA supplements are equivalent"

Incomplete proteins do not support muscle protein synthesis as well as complete proteins. BCAAs
provide the leucine trigger but not the building blocks. Whey contains BCAAs plus everything else, at
a lower cost per gram than most BCAA products.

## "Glutamine peptides boost immunity"

Whey contains glutamic acid and some glutamine, but clinical evidence for glutamine supplementation
in healthy athletes is weak. Marketing "glutamine peptides" as a separate benefit is not supported.

## "Natural flavours are toxins"

"Natural flavour" is a broad regulatory category, not a specific hazard. Some people prefer products
without them; that is a preference, not a safety requirement.

## "Grass-fed whey is a different protein"

The amino acid profile is essentially identical. The differences are in the fat fraction, which is
largely removed in isolate. See doc 13.

## "Organic whey is cleaner"

Organic certification restricts farming inputs, not heavy metals. Independent testing found organic
protein powders had **more** lead than conventional, driven by plant ingredients [CLP 2025]. For whey
specifically, organic does not predict lower contaminant levels.

## "Whey causes inflammation"

Dairy can be pro-inflammatory in some individuals, and whey has been shown to increase IGF-1 modestly,
but controlled trials generally show neutral or favourable effects on inflammatory markers. There is
no evidence of a general pro-inflammatory effect in healthy people.

## "You need to cycle whey"

No mechanism, no evidence. It is a food.

## "Women shouldn't use whey"

No. Whey is a protein; it does not masculinise. The testosterone and estradiol data above are
reassuring, and resistance training plus adequate protein benefits women at least as much as men for
bone, lean mass and function.

## Bottom line

Whey's real hormonal effects are modest and physiological: a transient insulin rise, a small IGF-1
increase, and no change in estradiol. Acne is the one area where a genuine individual susceptibility
may exist, and the controlled evidence is mixed. Almost everything else in circulation (kidney damage, liver
toxicity, estrogenic effects, bone loss, "only 30 g per meal") is either false or
applies only to people with specific diseases.
