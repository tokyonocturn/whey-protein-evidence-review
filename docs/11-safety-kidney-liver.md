# Long-term safety: kidney, liver and high protein intake

This is where most whey misinformation lives. The short version: **high protein is safe in healthy
people with healthy kidneys and livers, and potentially harmful in people with existing kidney
disease.** The two populations are not interchangeable, and conflating them produces both unnecessary
fear and genuine risk.

## What the evidence shows in healthy people

### Kidney function

The largest systematic review and meta-analysis of RCTs found that higher protein intakes produced a
trivially higher post-intervention GFR but **no difference in the change in GFR** over time; the
authors concluded that higher protein intakes do not adversely influence kidney function in healthy
adults [S10].

A 2026 meta-analysis of 22 RCTs (1,044 participants) found high-protein diets increased eGFR
(standardised mean difference 0.39) without any change in serum creatinine and without consistent
biochemical evidence of kidney injury [S11]. The authors caution that most trials were short and used
creatinine-based estimates, which are affected by protein intake and muscle mass.

A one-year randomised crossover study in 14 resistance-trained men consuming about **3.3 g/kg/day**
found no harmful changes in kidney or liver markers, lipids, or body composition [S08]. The ISSN
position stand notes that controlled studies up to 2.5-3.3 g/kg/day for up to a year have not shown
harm in healthy exercising people [S09].

### Liver

Liver enzymes (AST, ALT) have not shown clinically meaningful changes in high-protein trials,
including the one-year 3.3 g/kg study [S08]. Isolated case reports of elevated enzymes in bodybuilders
usually involve supplements, anabolic steroids, or underlying disease, not protein per se.

### The hyperfiltration debate

A 2024 review argues that chronic high protein intake causes glomerular hyperfiltration and may
contribute to CKD over decades, even in healthy people [S12]. This is a hypothesis, not established
causation. The counter-evidence is that populations with habitually high protein intakes (e.g. some
bodybuilders, traditional meat-heavy diets) do not show epidemic kidney failure, and the RCT data
show adaptation rather than injury.

The fair summary: **a transient, reversible increase in GFR is a normal physiological response to a
protein load.** Whether decades of very high intake (above about 2.5 g/kg/day) are truly neutral is
not settled, because no long-term (>5 year) randomised trial exists. Moderate high protein
(1.2-2.2 g/kg/day) is well supported as safe.

## People with existing kidney disease

This is where the advice reverses. KDIGO 2024 recommends:

- **0.8 g/kg/day** for adults with CKD G3-G5 (Recommendation 3.3.1.1, grade 2C).
- **Avoid protein intake above 1.3 g/kg/day** in adults with CKD at risk of progression.
- Consider a very-low-protein diet (0.3-0.4 g/kg/day) supplemented with essential amino acids or
  ketoacid analogues, under close supervision, in those at risk of kidney failure.
- Do not prescribe low-protein diets in metabolically unstable people [S13].

Other clinical guidance refines this by phenotype:

| Population | Protein target |
|---|---|
| Healthy adults | 0.8 g/kg/day |
| Non-dialysis diabetic CKD | 0.6-0.8 g/kg/day |
| Non-dialysis non-diabetic CKD | 0.55-0.6 g/kg/day |
| Advanced CKD (selected, supervised) | 0.3-0.4 g/kg/day plus ketoacids |
| Maintenance dialysis | 1.1-1.2 g/kg/day |
| Acute kidney injury | 1.1-1.5 g/kg/day temporarily |
| CKD with protein-energy wasting | 1.1-1.3 g/kg/day |

Note the dialysis row: once on dialysis, protein needs **rise**, because dialysis removes amino
acids. "Kidney disease means low protein" is an oversimplification.

A whey supplement is not inherently forbidden in CKD, but it must be counted in the total and
discussed with a renal dietitian. Protein source matters less than total amount, phosphorus and
potassium load.

## People with liver disease

The old advice to restrict protein in cirrhosis and hepatic encephalopathy was wrong and is now
explicitly reversed:

- **EASL 2019:** protein intake of **1.2-1.5 g/kg/day** in cirrhosis to prevent and reverse muscle
  loss [S14].
- **AASLD 2021:** **1.2-1.5 g/kg ideal body weight/day**, and **protein should not be restricted in
  hepatic encephalopathy** [S15].
- Critically ill patients with cirrhosis: 1.2-2.0 g/kg/day.
- Children with chronic liver disease: up to 4 g/kg/day in one study.

Cirrhosis is a protein-catabolic state. Restricting protein worsens sarcopenia and outcomes.

## Other safety considerations

- **Hydration and kidney stones.** Higher protein intakes increase urinary calcium and may slightly
  increase stone risk in susceptible people. Observational evidence does not show that high protein
  specifically triggers stones in healthy people, though hydration and calcium intake matter. A
  history of calcium oxalate stones warrants medical advice, not necessarily protein restriction.
- **Gout.** Whey is low in purines; dairy protein is associated with *lower* gout risk. This is not a
  concern.
- **Bone.** Higher protein intake is associated with higher BMD and lower hip fracture risk in
  cohorts, and supplementation does not harm bone [S42, S43]. The acid-ash hypothesis is not
  supported at normal intakes.
- **Digestive tolerance.** See doc 06.
- **Allergy.** See doc 06. Whey is contraindicated in cow's milk protein allergy.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Whey is a food protein and is fine in normal amounts. Avoid
  proprietary blends and high-dose herbal additives. Total protein needs rise modestly in pregnancy
  (about 1.1 g/kg/day in the second half).
- **Children and adolescents.** Whey is a food; protein needs are met by diet in most cases. Avoid
  supplements marketed with performance claims, and avoid creatine-containing blends.
- **Bariatric surgery.** Protein targets are high (60-80 g/day or more) but rapidly digested whey may
  cause dumping; follow the surgical team's guidance.

## What "excessive" actually looks like

There is no evidence-based upper limit for healthy adults. Practical guardrails:

- **1.2-2.2 g/kg/day:** well supported, no safety signal.
- **2.2-3.3 g/kg/day:** studied for up to a year without harm in small trials; no added benefit for
  most people; can displace fibre and micronutrients.
- **Above 3.3 g/kg/day:** no long-term data; not recommended.
- **Any intake with CKD, liver failure, urea cycle disorders, or before bariatric surgery:** medical
  supervision required.

## Bottom line

For healthy people, high protein, including whey, is safe up to at least 2.2 g/kg/day and has been
studied without harm up to about 3.3 g/kg/day for a year. The kidney-risk claim applies to people who
already have kidney disease, and to them it matters a great deal. In cirrhosis, protein should be
increased, not restricted. If you have kidney or liver disease, the number that matters is your
total intake under medical supervision, not which brand of whey you buy.
