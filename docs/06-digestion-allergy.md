# Lactose, digestion, allergy and intolerance

Three different problems get collapsed into "whey bothers my stomach": lactose malabsorption, cow's
milk protein allergy, and non-specific gastrointestinal symptoms from additives, dose or speed of
consumption. They have different causes and different solutions. Getting the distinction right is
the difference between buying the right product and giving up on whey unnecessarily.

## Lactose in whey products

Lactose is a sugar. Its amount depends on the whey grade, not the brand:

| Ingredient | Protein | Lactose by weight | Lactose in a 30 g scoop |
|---|---|---|---|
| WPC 34 | 34-36% | 48-55% | about 14-16 g |
| WPC 50 | 50-52% | 33-37% | about 10-11 g |
| WPC 80 | 80-82% | 4-8% | about 1.2-2.4 g |
| WPI | 90-92% | 0.5-1.0% | about 0.15-0.3 g |
| Hydrolysate | varies | matches base whey | depends on base |

Industry specifications from the American Dairy Products Institute and the US Dairy Export Council.
A finished flavoured powder also contains flavourings and sometimes added dairy fractions, so the
label is the final word; "whey protein isolate" as the first ingredient is the reliable signal.

**The practical point:** WPC 80 at 1.2-2.4 g lactose per scoop is a small dose. Most lactose
malabsorbers tolerate **12-15 g** of lactose in one sitting, roughly a cup of milk. A WPC shake is
therefore usually fine even for people who avoid milk. WPI is effectively lactose-free for practical
purposes but is **not** certified lactose-free, and "isolate" does not guarantee zero.

## How common is lactose intolerance?

- About **68% of the world's population** has lactose malabsorption; about **36% of people in the
  United States** [NIDDK].
- Prevalence is highest in East Asian, West African, Hispanic/Latino, Native American and
  African-American populations, and lowest in people of Northern European descent.
- Malabsorption is not the same as intolerance: many people with reduced lactase have no symptoms.

Symptoms (bloating, gas, cramps, diarrhoea) typically begin 30 minutes to 2 hours after ingestion and
dose-depend on the amount of lactose and on what it is consumed with.

## Cow's milk protein allergy is a different disease

Cow's milk protein allergy (CMPA) is an **immune** reaction to milk proteins, most commonly casein
and the whey proteins beta-lactoglobulin and alpha-lactalbumin. It is not lactose intolerance, and
lactose-free products are irrelevant to it.

- Prevalence: about **2-6% of children**, with most outgrowing it; confirmed CMPA in adults is
  **uncommon** (well under 1%), though self-reported "milk allergy" is far more common than confirmed
  allergy.
- IgE-mediated reactions occur within minutes to 2 hours: urticaria, angioedema, wheezing,
  vomiting, and in severe cases anaphylaxis. Beta-lactoglobulin is associated with more severe
  reactions.
- Non-IgE reactions are delayed (2-72 hours): eczema, reflux, diarrhoea, blood in stool, poor growth
  in infants.

**Critical implication:** whey protein isolate is **not hypoallergenic**. It contains beta-lactoglobulin
and alpha-lactalbumin. A person with diagnosed CMPA must avoid all whey products, including
hydrolysates, unless a clinician has confirmed tolerance. Extensively hydrolysed infant formulas are
a medical product, not the same as a sports whey hydrolysate.

This is also why "whey isolate is dairy-free" claims are false and dangerous. Whey is dairy.

## Non-specific gut symptoms

Many people who blame lactose are actually reacting to something else:

1. **Sugar alcohols.** Maltitol, sorbitol, xylitol and erythritol are fermentable and can cause
   gas, bloating and laxative effects. Erythritol is better tolerated than maltitol but still causes
   symptoms in some people at 20-30 g. A protein scoop rarely contains enough to matter, but
   "low-carb" and "keto" products sometimes do.
2. **Gums and fibres.** Xanthan, guar, acacia, inulin and chicory root are FODMAPs or fermentable
   fibres. They improve texture and mouthfeel; they also feed gut bacteria. Inulin in particular can
   cause gas at 5-10 g.
3. **Dose and speed.** 50 g of protein in one shake, drunk fast, causes symptoms in many people
   regardless of source. Split it.
4. **Concentrate fat content.** WPC has more fat than WPI; for people with fat malabsorption or
   gallbladder issues, isolate may be better tolerated.
5. **Added enzymes and probiotics.** Some products add lactase or proteases; evidence for benefit in
   otherwise healthy people is weak, and they add cost.
6. **Flavours and sweeteners.** Some people report headaches or GI symptoms from sucralose or
   acesulfame K; the evidence for a general effect is weak, but individual reactions exist.

## A practical elimination ladder

If whey causes symptoms, work through this in order rather than abandoning protein powder:

1. **Halve the dose** and split it into two servings. Symptoms often resolve.
2. **Switch to WPI** (not a blend). This removes most lactose and some fat.
3. **Switch to an unflavoured, unsweetened WPI** with no gums. This removes additives.
4. **Try a hydrolysate** if isolate still bothers you; some people tolerate it better, though the
   evidence is anecdotal.
5. **Try a non-dairy protein** (soy, pea/rice blend, egg white) if symptoms persist.
6. **See a clinician** if symptoms include rash, wheeze, swelling, blood in stool or weight loss.
   That is not intolerance.

## If you have kidney or liver disease

Protein intake may need to be modified, but the source matters less than the total. Do not
self-manage; see doc 11 for the guideline numbers and why they differ from athlete recommendations.

## Bottom line

- Most people who react badly to whey are reacting to lactose or additives, not to whey protein
  itself.
- WPC 80 is low-lactose enough for most malabsorbers; WPI is lower still.
- Cow's milk protein allergy is an immune disease and requires avoiding all whey, including isolate
  and hydrolysate, unless a clinician says otherwise.
- "Dairy-free" whey does not exist. "Lactose-free" whey does, but it is still milk protein.
