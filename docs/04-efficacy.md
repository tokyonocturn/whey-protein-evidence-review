# Does whey actually work? Muscle, strength, recovery and health

The honest answer is: **whey works, but mostly as a convenient way to hit a protein target, not as a
drug.** The effect size of protein supplementation on muscle is real, modest, and conditional on
resistance training and on whether you were already eating enough protein.

## Muscle mass and strength

The best single reference is Morton et al. 2018, a systematic review and meta-analysis of 49 trials
and 1,863 participants [S01]:

- Fat-free mass: **+0.30 kg** (95% CI 0.09-0.52) with protein supplementation versus control.
- One-repetition maximum strength: **+2.49 kg** (95% CI 0.64-4.33).
- Muscle fibre cross-sectional area: +310 µm²; mid-femur CSA +7.2 mm².
- **Breakpoint at 1.62 g/kg/day** (95% CI 1.03-2.20): beyond that, no further fat-free mass gain.
- The effect was smaller with increasing age and larger in resistance-trained people.

A dose-response meta-analysis of 105 articles and 5,402 participants found that each additional
0.1 g/kg/day of protein added **0.39 kg** of lean body mass below a total intake of 1.3 g/kg/day, and
only **0.12 kg** above it [S02]. A separate dose-response analysis of strength found gains of 0.72% per
0.1 g/kg/day of total protein **up to 1.5 g/kg/day, with no further gain beyond** [S03].

Put plainly: over 8-12 weeks of consistent training, protein supplementation adds a few hundred grams
of lean mass and a couple of kilos on a lift, on average. That is worth having. It is not
transformative, and it is not specific to whey.

## Whey versus other proteins

Whey's acute advantage is well documented. With 10 g of essential amino acids, whey hydrolysate
raised mixed muscle protein synthesis about 93% more than casein at rest and 122% more after
resistance exercise; it was also 31% higher than soy after exercise [S05]. That is a large acute
difference.

The catch is that acute MPS is a surrogate. When total daily protein is matched, long-term trials
rarely show whey beating other complete proteins for hypertrophy. A 2026 network meta-analysis of
protein sources ranked native whey, pea, whole egg and mycoprotein highly for acute MPS, but only
whey reached statistical significance and certainty was low to moderate. The practical reading:
**whey is the best-studied fast protein and a reliable choice, but "whey or nothing" is not supported
once total protein is adequate.**

## Recovery

Protein after resistance exercise supports repair, but the "30-minute anabolic window" is much wider
than supplement marketing implies. Reviews of protein distribution put total daily intake well ahead
of timing [S04]. Protein supplementation
modestly reduces markers of muscle damage and soreness in some trials; effects are small and
inconsistent.

Hydrolysed whey has the most interesting recovery data: trials reviewed in [S06] report faster
recovery of peak torque after eccentric exercise versus intact whey. It has not been replicated at a scale that
justifies a 30-50% price premium for most people.

## Older adults

Ageing produces anabolic resistance: older muscle needs more protein and more leucine per meal to
respond. Expert groups recommend **1.0-1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older adults** and **1.2-1.5
g/kg/day during illness**, with 25-30 g of high-quality protein per meal [S04, S16]. Whey is well suited
because it is leucine-dense, easy to chew and digest, and cheap per gram of protein. This is one of
the clearest use cases for a whey supplement: a person who cannot eat enough protein from food.

## Health outcomes beyond muscle

Whey is not just protein; it is a dairy fraction with bioactive peptides. The clinical literature
shows small but real effects:

- **Blood pressure:** a meta-analysis of 18 RCTs (1,177 participants) found systolic blood pressure
  fell **1.54 mmHg**; diastolic fell only at doses above 30 g/day [S40]. Clinically trivial for most,
  meaningful at population scale, not a treatment.
- **Lipids:** a 2025 meta-analysis of 21 RCTs found LDL cholesterol fell in adults under 50 and when
  whey was combined with exercise; total cholesterol fell with exercise; there was no clinically
  relevant blood pressure or insulin-resistance effect [S39].
- **Postprandial glucose:** whey taken before a carbohydrate meal lowered peak glucose by **1.4
  mmol/L** across 16 crossover trials, with greater effects in type 2 diabetes [S41]. This is the
  best-supported "functional" use of whey outside muscle.
- **Satiety and weight:** whey is more satiating per calorie than carbohydrate, partly via GLP-1 and
  CCK. Effects on weight loss are modest and not clearly superior to other proteins.
- **Bone:** higher protein intake is associated with higher bone mineral density and lower hip
  fracture risk in cohorts, and short supplementation trials do not change BMD [S42, S43]. There is
  no evidence that whey harms bone, and the old "protein leaches calcium" claim is not supported at
  normal intakes.

None of these effects require whey specifically. They require adequate protein, and whey is a
convenient delivery vehicle.

## When a supplement adds nothing

If someone already eats 1.6 g/kg/day of protein from food, adding whey does not add muscle [S01].
This is the single most important practical point in the entire review. Supplements are a solution
to a **protein deficit**, not a performance drug. A 80 kg person needs about 128 g/day at 1.6 g/kg;
most people who lift and eat three meals with a protein source at each already reach that.

## What whey does not do

- It does not override insufficient training, sleep or calories.
- It does not preferentially burn fat; protein's thermic effect and satiety help, but the effect is
  small.
- It does not "boost metabolism" in a meaningful way.
- It does not replace a varied diet; it is one food.
- It does not make a non-responder respond. Some people gain little muscle on any program.

## Bottom line

Whey is a high-quality, convenient, well-studied protein with a modest, reliable effect on
training adaptations when it closes a protein gap. The effect is smaller than marketing suggests and
not unique to whey. Its clearest evidence-based niches are: hitting a daily protein target
conveniently, older adults at risk of inadequate intake, and pre-meal glucose lowering in people with
or at risk of type 2 diabetes.
