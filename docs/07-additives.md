# Sweeteners, gums, fillers, flavours and other additives

A flavoured whey powder is not just protein. A typical tub contains protein, cocoa or flavour
powder, sweeteners, an emulsifier (usually lecithin), a thickener (usually xanthan or cellulose gum),
salt, and sometimes enzymes, probiotics, vitamins or free amino acids. Most of these are benign. A
few are worth avoiding, and a few are markers of a product that is cutting corners.

## What is actually in the tub

| Additive class | Common examples | Function | Evidence-based concern |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intense sweeteners | Sucralose, acesulfame K, steviol glycosides, monk fruit, aspartame (rare in powders) | Sweetness without sugar | ADI-based safety; microbiome signals in animal studies; individual sensitivity |
| Sugar alcohols | Erythritol, maltitol, xylitol, sorbitol | Bulk sweetness, low calories | GI upset at dose; erythritol platelet signal (contested) |
| Gums and fibres | Xanthan, guar, acacia, cellulose gum, inulin, chicory root | Thickness, mouthfeel, fibre claims | Fermentable; gas and bloating in sensitive people |
| Emulsifiers | Soy lecithin, sunflower lecithin | Mixability | Allergen declaration (soy); no general safety concern |
| Fillers | Maltodextrin, dextrose, modified starch | Bulk, flavour carrier, "mass gainer" calories | Glycaemic load; dilutes protein density |
| Flavours | "Natural and artificial flavours", cocoa, salt | Taste | Undisclosed composition; cocoa carries heavy metals |
| Enzymes | Lactase, protease | Digestibility claims | Weak evidence in healthy people |
| Free amino acids | Glycine, taurine, glutamine, creatine, BCAA | "Nitrogen spiking", taste, marketing | Inflates protein number; see doc 02 |
| Colourants | Titanium dioxide (rare now), caramel colour | Appearance | Titanium dioxide no longer authorised as a food additive in the EU |

## Sweeteners: the ADI maths

Acceptable Daily Intakes are set with large safety margins, typically 100-fold below the highest dose
with no observed effect in animals. The relevant question is not "is it toxic" but "how close does a
scoop get you to the ADI".

| Sweetener | ADI (FDA) | ADI for a 70 kg adult | Typical amount in a flavoured scoop (estimate) | Share of ADI |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sucralose | 5 mg/kg/day | 350 mg/day | 30-80 mg | 9-23% |
| Acesulfame K | 15 mg/kg/day | 1,050 mg/day | 20-60 mg | 2-6% |
| Steviol glycosides (as steviol) | 4 mg/kg/day | 280 mg/day | 30-100 mg | 11-36% |
| Reb A | 12 mg/kg/day | 840 mg/day | 30-100 mg | 4-12% |
| Erythritol | No numerical ADI; laxative threshold is the critical effect | - | 0-10 g | - |
| Maltitol | Laxative threshold around 20-30 g | - | 0-10 g | - |

Scoop sweetener amounts are **estimates** based on typical use levels; brands do not disclose them.
Even at the top of the range, a single daily scoop is well under the ADI. Heavy users (3-4
scoops/day) plus diet soda plus sugar-free gum can approach it, which is worth knowing but not
alarming.

### Sucralose

The most common sweetener in whey. EFSA re-evaluated it in 2025-26 and kept the ADI at 15 mg/kg/day
(EFSA's ADI is higher than FDA's 5 mg/kg/day). Animal studies show sucralose can alter gut microbiota
composition; human evidence is limited and inconsistent [S29]. Heating sucralose at high
temperatures can generate chlorinated by-products, which is a reason not to bake with it, not a
reason to avoid it in a cold shake.

### Acesulfame K

Usually paired with sucralose to round out the sweetness. EFSA raised the ADI to **15 mg/kg/day** in
2025 [S27]. It is one of the most studied sweeteners and is not genotoxic or carcinogenic at
ADI-level exposure. Some in vitro and bioreactor studies suggest it can disturb microbial community
structure; human clinical relevance is unclear.

### Steviol glycosides and monk fruit

Plant-derived. Stevia (steviol glycosides) has an ADI of 4 mg/kg/day expressed as steviol equivalents,
and Reb A 12 mg/kg/day. Monk fruit (mogroside) has no established ADI because it has no observed
toxicity. These are the sweeteners most likely to be chosen by people who want to avoid synthetic
additives, and the evidence does not show harm at normal intake. They do have a licorice-like
aftertaste that some people dislike.

### Erythritol

A four-carbon sugar alcohol that is about 70% as sweet as sugar and mostly excreted unchanged.
EFSA's 2023 re-evaluation concluded that the available evidence **does not demonstrate a causal
relationship** between dietary erythritol and cardiovascular risk, while noting observational
associations [S26]. A 2023 Cleveland Clinic intervention study found erythritol ingestion enhanced
platelet reactivity in healthy volunteers [S25], and subsequent commentary has urged caution. This is
a genuine open question, not a settled harm. Erythritol is also the best-tolerated sugar alcohol
because most of it is absorbed and excreted; maltitol and xylitol are far more laxative.

## Gums and fibres

Xanthan gum, guar gum, acacia fibre and cellulose gum are used at 0.2-0.5 g per serving to improve
body and suspend cocoa. They are generally safe and are also used in gluten-free baking.

Two caveats:

- **Fermentable fibres cause gas.** Inulin and chicory root in particular are FODMAPs. If a product
  markets "prebiotic fibre", it may also cause bloating.
- **Gums can slow gastric emptying**, which is usually harmless but may worsen reflux in some people.

Carrageenan, a sulfated polysaccharide from seaweed, is used in some ready-to-drink shakes and a few
powders. Animal studies have raised concerns about intestinal inflammation; human evidence at food
levels is weak. It is not a reason to avoid a product, but it is a legitimate preference to avoid.

## Lecithin

Soy or sunflower lecithin (about 0.2-0.5 g per serving) is an emulsifier that helps powder disperse
in water. It is the most common reason a whey product declares **soy** as an allergen, even though
the protein is dairy. Highly refined soy lecithin contains very little soy protein, and most soy
allergic people tolerate it, but the label declaration is legally required and should be respected.
Sunflower lecithin is the alternative for people avoiding soy.

## Fillers and protein density

Maltodextrin, dextrose and modified starch add bulk, sweetness and calories. In a "mass gainer" they
are the point. In a protein powder they dilute the protein:

- A 47.8%-protein blend (BSN Syntha-6 in our dataset) delivers 22 g of protein in a 46 g scoop with
  15 g of carbohydrate and 6 g of fat, at 227 kcal per 25 g of protein.
- An 84%-protein isolate delivers 25 g in a 30 g scoop at about 120 kcal per 25 g of protein.

Neither is "bad". They are different products for different purposes. The problem is when a
low-density product is priced like a high-density one.

## "Natural flavours" and proprietary blends

"Natural flavour" is an FDA-permitted umbrella term covering dozens of compounds. It tells you the
source is natural, not what the chemicals are. For most people this is irrelevant. For people with
allergies or sensitivities, it is a reason to prefer unflavoured products or brands that disclose
flavour sources.

Proprietary blends are a bigger issue: if a "muscle matrix" lists 5 g of a blend without individual
amounts, you cannot tell whether any ingredient is present at an effective dose. Avoid blends when
the ingredients matter.

## Amino acid spiking, revisited

Adding glycine, taurine, glutamine, creatine or BCAAs inflates the nitrogen-derived protein number.
It is cheap, legal in many jurisdictions, and misleading. Signs:

- Individual amino acids in the ingredient list of a "whey protein".
- A protein number that does not match the protein density: for example, 30 g of protein in a 43 g
  scoop (69.8%) in a product that also lists taurine and creatine.
- A price per gram noticeably below the market.

Regulators can act: 21 CFR 101.36 prohibits declaring protein on labels of products containing only
individual amino acids, and courts have allowed misleading-labelling claims to proceed [see doc 14].

## What to actually avoid

| Priority | Avoid | Why |
|---|---|---|
| High | Products with individual amino acids in a whey product, if you are paying for whey | You are paying whey prices for glycine |
| High | Any whey product if you have diagnosed cow's milk protein allergy | Contains milk protein |
| Medium | Maltitol-heavy products if you are GI-sensitive | Laxative effect |
| Medium | Inulin/chicory "fibre" products if you bloat easily | Fermentable |
| Medium | Chocolate flavours for daily use if you are concerned about cadmium/lead | Cocoa is the main contaminant driver |
| Low | Sucralose/ace-K | Well under ADI for most users; preference, not hazard |
| Low | Soy lecithin | Unless you have soy allergy |
| Low | Gums | Unless you are FODMAP-sensitive |

## Bottom line

The additive load of a typical whey powder is low and the doses are small. Sweeteners are within
ADI by a wide margin for normal use; gums and fibres are the most likely cause of bloating;
maltodextrin dilutes protein density; and individual amino acids in a whey product are a commercial
red flag rather than a safety one. The single most useful label habit is to check the ingredient list
for free amino acids and the protein density (protein grams divided by scoop grams).
