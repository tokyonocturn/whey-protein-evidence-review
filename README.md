# Whey Protein Powders: An Independent Scientific Investigation

**A source-first review of 16 leading whey products, 56 flavours, 46 studies, and the independent laboratory evidence on protein quality, dosing, additives, contaminants, safety and cost.**

Research cut-off: **18 November 2026**. Prices, formulations and test results are dated in the datasets. Protein powders are reformulated without notice, so the tub in your hand overrides this repository.

> This is an evidence review, not medical advice. Kidney disease, liver disease, pregnancy, bariatric surgery and cow's milk protein allergy change the answer completely; see [safety](docs/11-safety-kidney-liver.md).

---

## Bottom line first

| Question | Short answer |
|---|---|
| Is whey a good protein? | **Yes.** DIAAS 1.09 for isolate, 0.97 for concentrate, complete amino acid profile, about 2.6 g leucine per 25 g. Among the best food proteins measured. |
| Does it build muscle? | **Modestly.** About +0.30 kg fat-free mass and +2.49 kg on a lift over 8–12 weeks of training, *if* it closes a protein gap. Gains stop around 1.6 g/kg/day. |
| Is isolate better than concentrate? | **Only for lactose and calories.** Same amino acids, marginally higher DIAAS, 5–15x less lactose, 30–100% more expensive per gram of protein. |
| Is hydrolysate worth the premium? | **Not for most people.** Faster absorption; similar muscle protein synthesis in a direct comparison. |
| How much protein? | **1.6 g/kg/day** (range 1.2–2.2), spread over 3–5 meals of 25–30 g. |
| Is high protein safe? | **Yes in healthy people**, studied without harm to ~3.3 g/kg/day for a year. **No in chronic kidney disease**, 0.8 g/kg/day, avoid >1.3. |
| Are heavy metals a problem? | **Yes, mostly in plant and chocolate products.** 28% of whey powders exceeded California's Prop 65 lead benchmark vs 77% of plant and 79% of organic. |
| Do certifications mean anything? | **Yes, but only specific ones.** Informed Sport (every batch) and NSF Certified for Sport are the meaningful programmes. |
| What should most people buy? | A certified, vanilla or unflavoured, 70–85%-protein blend or concentrate at the best verified price per 25 g of protein. |

## The ten findings that matter

**1. The form of whey matters far less than the marketing implies.** Concentrate, isolate and hydrolysate deliver the same amino acids. Isolate is filtered further to remove lactose and fat; hydrolysate is pre-digested for faster absorption. In the one direct MPS comparison, hydrolysate and intact whey produced statistically similar responses (+67% vs +57%) [S44].

**2. Protein quality is genuinely high, and DIAAS finally shows it.** PDCAAS truncates at 1.00, so every decent protein scores identically. DIAAS does not: WPI 1.09, WPC 0.97, soy 0.90, pea 0.82, rice 0.37 [S17, S18]. Whey is leucine-dense at roughly 10–13% of protein, about 2.6 g per 25 g serving.

**3. The muscle effect is real, small, and conditional.** Morton's meta-analysis of 49 trials: +0.30 kg fat-free mass, +2.49 kg strength, with a breakpoint at **1.62 g/kg/day** [S01]. If you already eat enough protein, whey adds nothing.

**4. More protein is not more muscle.** A dose-response analysis found strength gains plateau at 1.5 g/kg/day [S03]; another found each extra 0.1 g/kg/day added 0.39 kg of lean mass below 1.3 g/kg/day but only 0.12 kg above it [S02]. Above ~2.2 g/kg/day, the extra is oxidised or excreted.

**5. Whey is one of the cleaner protein sources, but not clean by default.** 28% of whey products exceeded Prop 65's lead benchmark in the Clean Label Project's 2025 study, versus 77% of plant proteins [S30]. Consumer Reports found plant proteins averaged **nine times** the lead of dairy [S31].

**6. Chocolate flavours are the contaminant driver.** 65% of chocolate protein powders exceeded Prop 65 for lead, and chocolate has been reported to carry up to 110x the cadmium of vanilla [S30]. The Clean Sixteen (products non-detect for all four heavy metals) is dominated by vanilla and unflavoured SKUs.

**7. Low-lead products exist at mass-market prices.** Consumer Reports' January 2026 follow-up measured Clean Simple Eats at 0.21 µg and Walmart's Equate at 0.27 µg of lead per serving (see [contaminants](docs/09-contaminants.md)). Contamination is a manufacturing choice, not an inevitability.

**8. Most "whey" products are not spiked; some are.** Adding glycine, taurine, creatine or free amino acids inflates the nitrogen-derived protein number. Courts allowed spiking claims against CVS and NBTY/Body Fortress to proceed; neither produced a public judgment (see [doc 14](docs/14-manufacturing-recalls-regulation.md)). Check for individual amino acids in the ingredient list.

**9. The kidney claim applies to sick kidneys, not healthy ones.** Meta-analyses show no adverse effect on kidney function in healthy adults [S10, S11]; KDIGO recommends 0.8 g/kg/day and avoiding >1.3 g/kg/day in CKD G3–G5 [S13]. In cirrhosis, protein should be **increased** to 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day, not restricted [S14, S15].

**10. Cost varies 5.2x for the same nutrient.** From **$0.77** to **$4.03 per 25 g of protein** in our sample. Optimum Nutrition Gold Standard, the market leader, sits at **$1.51**, cheaper than most premium isolates and better tested than most of them.

## Products with real caveats

- **Body Fortress Super Advanced Whey**: cheapest per labelled gram ($0.77/25 g), but 69.8% protein density and a documented spiking allegation. Its vanilla SKU is clean; its protein number is contested.
- **BSN Syntha-6**: 47.8% protein density, 227 kcal per 25 g of protein. A milkshake, not an efficient protein.
- **Chocolate SKUs used daily**: cocoa carries cadmium and lead. Rotate, don't rely.
- **Any whey product for diagnosed cow's milk protein allergy**: isolate is not hypoallergenic.

## Data and reproducibility

The datasets live in `data/`. Start with `data/products.csv` (label composition) and `data/prices.csv` (dated prices), then read `data/derived_costs.csv` for the computed rankings. Contamination evidence is in `data/contaminants.csv`, and full citations with URLs are in `docs/references.md`.

Every cost figure in the documentation is generated, not typed:

```bash
python analysis/compute_costs.py   # writes data/derived_costs.csv
python analysis/validate.py        # 893 checks; exits non-zero on failure
python analysis/check_docs.py      # links, citations and published figures
python analysis/check_audit.py     # every citation marker has a verdict
python analysis/check_refs.py      # optional: live link-rot and DOI check (network)
```

The citation audit covers every in-text marker, not a sample: 114 instances across the README and the 17 documents, of which 98 are supported by the cited source's abstract or open-access text and 16 are explicitly unverified with the reason recorded. `analysis/check_audit.py` fails if a marker is added or edited without a verdict, so the coverage cannot silently decay.

`validate.py` recomputes each cost independently, verifies protein never exceeds serving weight, checks protein density is physiologically plausible, verifies the amino acid table sums and that EAA/BCAA/leucine totals fall in published ranges, confirms every priced product has a label row and vice versa, checks that flavour rows reference real products, checks study IDs are sequential, and confirms every document and dataset referenced here exists.

## Method in one paragraph

Seventeen products were chosen for formulation spread, market presence, certification status and availability of independent test data. Efficacy and safety claims come from systematic reviews and RCTs, weighted above cohorts, which are weighted above laboratory reports and labels. Contamination findings are synthesised from the Clean Label Project's 2025 study (160 products, 35,862 analyses) and Consumer Reports' 2025–26 testing. Cost per 25 g of protein normalises across products with 20–30 g servings. No primary laboratory testing was commissioned, no brand reviewed this work, and there is no conflict of interest. Full detail in [methodology](docs/01-methodology.md).

## Limitations you should know before citing this

1. **No primary testing.** Contamination conclusions inherit the methods and limits of the cited labs.
2. **Advocacy data.** CLP and CR are independent of brands but use strict benchmarks and small samples; use them for ranking, not absolute risk.
3. **Labels are not lab-verified.** Independent surveys find 5–30% of products off-label to some degree.
4. **Prices are a snapshot.** November 2026, US market, one pack size per product.
5. **Flavour lists are partial for some brands.** Marked `partial` in the dataset.
6. **The evidence base is young, male and short.** Most trials are small, short, and funded by industry.

Details in [limitations](docs/17-limitations.md).
